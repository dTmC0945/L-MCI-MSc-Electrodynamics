2.5 Dirac Delta Function

2.5.1 A Mathematical Anomaly

Consider the following vector function:

v = 1 r2r^

At every location, v is directed radially outward which we can see in Fig. 2.10. Let’s calculate its divergence:

∇ ⁡ · v = 1 r2 ∂ ∂r (r2 1 r2 ) = 1 r2 ∂ ∂r (1) = 0

This is interesting as this calculation gives us an unforeseen solution. Let’s look at it closer. Suppose we integrate over a sphere of radius R, centred at the origin. The surface integral therefore is

∮ v · da = ∫ ( 1 R2r^) · (R2 sin⁡𝜃d𝜃dϕr^) = (∫ 0π sin⁡𝜃d𝜃) (∫ 02πdϕ) = 4π

But the volume integral (∫ ∇ ⁡ · vdτ), should be zero (0) if we assume the aforementioned calculation to be true.

Does this mean that the divergence theorem is false?

The source of the problem lies at the point r = 0, where v blows up. It is true that ∇ ⁡ · v = 0 everywhere except the origin, but right at the origin is the situation gets a little bit complicated.

Observe, the surface integral is independent of R. If the divergence theorem is to be true, we should expect

∫ ∇ ⁡ · vdτ = 4π,

for any non-zero vector and the origin.

This means the value of 4π must be coming from the point r = 0. Therefore, ∇ ⁡ · v has the unique property that it vanishes everywhere except at one point, and yet its integral is 4π.

Warning

No normal function behaves like that.

¶ Information 2.2: An Analogy with Density

To wrap our heads around this property think of density.

The density of a point particle is zero except at the exact location of the particle, and yet its integral is finite. Namely, the mass of the particle.

What we have stumbled upon is called the Dirac delta function. It arises in numerous branches of theoretical physics and plays a central role in the theory of electrodynamics.

2.5.2 The 1D Dirac Delta Function

The one-dimensional Dirac delta function (δ(x)), can be pictured as an infinitely high, infinitesimally narrow “spike”, with area 1.

That is to say:

δ(x) = { 0, ifx ≠ 0 ∞, if x = 0

and,

∫ −∞∞δ(x)dx = 1

In a strict sense of definition, δ(x) is NOT a function at all, as its value is not finite at x = 0. In literature it is known as a generalised function. [39] Objects extending the notion of functions on real or complex numbers. There is more than one recognised theory, for example the theory of distributions. Generalised functions are especially useful for treating discontinuous functions more like smooth functions, and describing discrete physical phenomena such as point charges.

If f(x) is some “ordinary” function, then the product (f(x)δ(x)) is zero everywhere except at x = 0. It follows that:

f(x)δ(x) = f(0)δ(x). (2.10)

Knowledge

The product is zero anyway except at x = 0. Based on this property, we may as well replace f(x) by the value it assumes at the origin.

Particularly

∫ −∞∞f(x)δ(x)dx = f(0) ∫ −∞∞δ(x)dx = f(0). (2.11)

Under an integral, then, the delta function “picks out” the value of f(x) at x = 0. [40] Here and below, the integral need not run from − ∞ to + ∞; it is sufficient that the domain extend across the delta function, and − 𝜖 to + 𝜖 would do as well. Of course, we can shift the spike from x = 0 to some other point, x = a:

δ(x−a) = { 0, ifx ≠ a ∞, if x = a with∫ −∞∞δ(x−a)dx = 1. (2.12)

which turns Eq. (2.10) into:

f(x)δ(x − a) = f(a)δ(x − a), (2.13)

and Eq. (2.11) generalises to:

∫ −∞∞f(x)δ(x − a)dx = f(a). (2.14)

While δ itself is not a proper function, integrals over δ are perfectly acceptable. In fact, it’s best to think of the delta function as something that is always intended for use under an integral sign. In particular, two expressions involving delta functions. say D1(x) and D2(x), are considered equal if:

∫ −∞∞f(x)D 1(x)dx = ∫ −∞∞f(x)D 2(x)dx, (2.15)

for all ordinary functions f(x).

2.5.3 The 3D Dirac Delta Function

Once we have defined the 1D Dirac, it is simple to generalise it to 3D with the following:

δ3 (r) = δ (x)δ (y)δ (z), (2.16)

As we can see, it similar to 1D, where 3D Dirac is zero everywhere except at (0,0,0), where it blows up. Its volume integral is 1:

∫ all spaceδ3(r)dτ = ∭ −∞∞δ(x)δ(y)δ(z)dxdydz = 1

And, the general form is:

∫ all spacef(r)δ3(r − a)dτ = f(a). (2.17)

As in the 1D case, integration with δ picks out the value of the function f at the location of the spike.

We can fix the paradox introduced in Section 2.5. Remember, the divergence of r^/r2 is zero everywhere except at the origin, however, its integral over any volume containing the origin is a constant.

These are precisely the defining conditions for the Dirac delta function; evidently

∇ ⁡ · (r^ r2 ) = 4πδ3 (r)

Or in a more general fashion:

∇ ⁡ · ({ηr^} {ηr}2 ) = 4πδ3 ({ηr^}) (2.18)

Differentiation here is with respect to r, while rr is held constant.
