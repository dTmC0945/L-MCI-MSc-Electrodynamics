2.4 Curvilinear Coordinates

Curvilinear coordinates are a generalized coordinate system in which coordinate lines can be curved. Unlike Cartesian systems that use straight, fixed axes, curvilinear coordinates feature basis vectors that change in direction or magnitude from point to point. Here we will have a look at the two most important ones which have numerous applications in engineering and physics problems.

2.4.1 Spherical Coordinate System

It is possible to label a point P in Cartesian coordinates (x,y,z), but sometimes it is more convenient to use spherical coordinates (r,𝜃,ϕ); r is the distance from the origin, 𝜃 is called the polar angle, and ϕ is the azimuthal angle. Their relation to Cartesian coordinates can be read from Fig. 2.9a.

x = rsin⁡𝜃cos⁡ϕ,y = rsin⁡𝜃sin⁡ϕ,z = rcos⁡𝜃.

Fig. 2.9a also shows three unit vectors, r^,𝜃^,ϕ^, pointing in the direction of increase of the corresponding coordinates.

They constitute an orthogonal basis set, [38] meaning mutually perpendicular. similar to x^,ŷ,ẑ, and any vector A can be expressed in terms of them, in the usual way:

A = (Ar) r^ + (A𝜃) 𝜃^ + (Aϕ) ϕ^

Here, Ar,A𝜃,Aϕ are the radial, polar, and azimuthal components of vector A. In terms of the Cartesian unit vectors:

r^ = (sin⁡𝜃cos⁡ϕ)r^ + (sin⁡𝜃sin⁡ϕ)𝜃^ + (cos⁡𝜃)ϕ^, 𝜃^ = (cos⁡𝜃cos⁡ϕ)r^ + (cos⁡𝜃sin⁡ϕ)𝜃^ + (− sin⁡𝜃)ϕ^, ϕ^ = (− sin⁡ϕ)r^ + (cos⁡ϕ)𝜃^ + (0)ϕ^.

An infinitesimal displacement in the r^ direction is simply dr, just as an infinitesimal element of length in the x^ direction is dx:

d lr = dr

On the other hand, an infinitesimal element of length in the 𝜃^ direction is NOT just d𝜃 but rather,

d l𝜃 = rd𝜃

Similarly, an infinitesimal element of length in the ϕ^ direction is

d lϕ = rsin⁡𝜃dϕ

Thus the general infinitesimal displacement dl is:

d l = (dr)r^ + (rd𝜃)𝜃^ + (rsin⁡𝜃)ϕ^

This plays the role dl = (dx)x^ + (dy)ŷ + (dz)ẑ plays in Cartesian coordinates. The infinitesimal volume element dτ, in spherical coordinates, is the product of the three (3) infinitesimal displacements:

d τ = dlrdl𝜃dlϕ = r2 sin⁡𝜃drd𝜃dϕ.
Warning

It is not possible to give a general expression for surface elements da, since these depend on the orientation of the surface. We simply have to analyse the geometry for any given case, which goes for Cartesian and curvilinear coordinates.

Integrating over the surface of a sphere, for instance, makes r constant, whereas 𝜃 and ϕ change:

d a 1 = dl𝜃dlϕr^ = r2 sin⁡𝜃d𝜃dϕr^

On the other hand, if the surface lies in the xy plane, making 𝜃 is constant, while r and ϕ vary:

d a 2 = dlrdlϕ𝜃^ = rdrdϕ𝜃^

Finally: r ranges from 0 to ∞, ϕ from 0 to 2π, and 𝜃 from 0 to π.

2.4.2 Cylindrical Coordinates

The cylindrical coordinates (s,ϕ,z) of a point P are defined in Fig. 2.9b. Observe that ϕ has the same meaning as in spherical coordinates, and z is the same as Cartesian; s is the distance to P from the z axis, whereas the spherical coordinate r is the distance from the origin. The relation to Cartesian coordinates is:

x = scos⁡ϕy = ssin⁡ϕz = z.

The unit vectors are:

ŝ = cos⁡ϕx^ + sin⁡ϕŷ ϕ^ = − sin⁡ϕx^ + cos⁡ϕŷ ẑ = ẑ

The infinitesimal displacements are

d ls = dsdlϕ = sdϕ,dlz = dz

which makes:

d l = dsŝ + sdϕphi^ + dzẑ.

and the volume element is

d τ′ = sdsdϕdz

The range of s is (0, ∞), ϕ is from 0 to 2pi and z is from − ∞ to + ∞
