4.3 The Auxiliary H Field

4.3.1 Ampère’s Law in Magnetized Materials

In the previous section, we found that the effect of magnetization is to establish bound currents Jb = ∇ ⁡ × M within the material and Kb = M × n^ on the surface. The field due to magnetization of the medium is just the field produced by these bound currents.

It is time we put everything together:

The field attributable to bound currents, plus the field due to everything else, which we will call the free current.

The free current might flow through wires embedded in the magnetized substance or, if the latter is a conductor, through the material itself.

Regardless of any event, the total current can be written as

J = Jb + Jf. (4.24)

There is nothing new in Eq. (4.24) as it is simply a convenience to separate the current into these two (2) parts,as they got there by quite different means:

  • The free current is there because somebody hooked up a wire to a battery. It involves actual transport of charge.

  • The bound current is there because of magnetization. It results from the collective behavior of many aligned atomic dipoles.

In view of Eq. (4.19) and Eq. (4.24) , Ampère’s law can be written as:

1 μ0 (∇ ⁡ × B) = J = Jf + Jb = Jf + (∇ ⁡ × M) or tidying them up∇ ⁡×( 1 μ0B − M) = Jf.

The quantity in parentheses is designated by the letter H:

H ≡ 1 μ0B − M. (4.25)

In terms of H, then, Ampère’s law reads

∇ ⁡ × H = Jf,in integral form∮ H · dl = Ifenc, (4.26)

where Ifenc is the total free current passing through the Amperian loop. H plays a role in magnetostatics similar to D in electrostatics:

Just as D allowed us to write Gauss’s law in terms of the free charge alone, H permits us to express Ampère’s law in terms of the free current alone.

Free current is what we control directly

Bound current, similar to bound charge, comes naturally. The material gets magnetized, and this results in bound currents.

We cannot turn them on or off independently, as we can free currents.

In applying Eq. (4.26) , all we need to worry about is the free current, which we know about because we put it there. In particular, when symmetry permits, we can calculate H immediately from Eq. (??) by the usual Ampère’s law methods.

Practicality of The Auxiliary Field

Turns out, H is a more useful quantity than D. In the laboratory, many scientist will be talking about H, [95] Even more often than B. but will almost never speak of D, as most will work with E instead.

The reason is this straightforward:

To build an electromagnet we run a certain current [96] As this is a current we are putting into the system we consider this free current. through a coil. The current is the thing we read on our measuring equipment, and this determines H; [97] Or if we want to get pedantic, the line integral of H. B depends on the specific materials we used and even, if iron is present, on the history of the magnet.

On the other hand, if we want to set up an electric field, we don’t plaster a known free charge on the plates of a parallel plate capacitor. Rather, we connect them to a battery of known voltage. It’s the potential difference we read on your dial, and that determines E. [98] Or rather, the line integral of E.

D depends on the details of the dielectric we’re using.

If it were easy to measure charge, and hard to measure potential, then we’d find experimentalists talking more about D instead of E. So the relative familiarity of H, as contrasted with D, derives from purely practical considerations. [99] This is why majority of engineering applications use the H field in their calculation instead of using B [†-1] Cheng, D. K. et al. Field and wave electromagnetics (Pearson Education India, 1989). Its just easier.

Theoretically, they’re on an equal footing.

Information 4.8: The Naming of the Field

Many authors call H, not B, the magnetic field. Then they have to invent a new word for B: the flux density, or magnetic induction . [100] an absurd choice, given the term already has at least two other meanings in electrodynamics. This would do nothing but add more to the confusion. B is indisputably the fundamental quantity, therefore we will continue to call it the magnetic field. H has no sensible name and therefore to keep it simple, just call it H.

4.3.2 A Deceptive Parallel

If we were to look at Eq. (4.26) closely, we will see that it looks just like Ampère’s original law Eq. (??) , with the exception that the total current is replaced by the free current, and B is replaced by μ0H.

Similar to the case of D, however, We need to be careful, as it does NOT say that

μ0H is just like B, only its source is Jf instead of J.

For the curl alone does not determine a vector field as we must also know the divergence. [101] This is one of the key ideas of Vector Field Theory which we covered in Vector Calculus. And whereas ∇ ⁡ · B = 0, the divergence of H is NOT, in general, zero.

In fact, from Eq. (4.25)

∇ ⁡ · H = −∇ ⁡ · M. (4.27)

Only when the divergence of M vanishes is the parallel between B and μ0H faithful.

A Thought Experiment

Consider the example of the bar magnet; a short cylinder of iron carrying a permanent uniform magnetization M parallel to its axis. In this case there is no free current anywhere, and a simple application of Eq. (4.26) might lead us to assume H being 0, and therefore assuming B = μ0M inside the magnet and B = 0 outside, which makes no physical sense. It is quite true that the ∇ ⁡ × H vanishes everywhere, but the divergence does NOT.

Approaching the Problem

When we are asked to find B or H in a problem involving magnetic materials, first try look for symmetry. If the problem permits cylindrical, plane, solenoidal, or toroidal symmetry, then we can get H directly from Eq. (4.26) by the usual Ampère’s law methods. [102] Evidently, in such cases ∇ ⁡ · M is automatically zero, given the free current alone determines the answer. If the requisite symmetry is absent, we’ll have to think of another approach, and in particular we cannotx assume that H is zero just because there is no free current in sight.

4.3.3 Boundary Conditions

The magnetostatic boundary conditions of Sect. 5.4.2 can be rewritten in terms of H and the free current. From Eq. (4.27) it follows that

Habove⊥ − H below⊥ = −(M above⊥ − M below⊥), (4.28)

whereas Eq. (??) says;

H above∥ − H below∥ = K f × n^. (4.29)

In the presence of materials, these are sometimes more useful than the corresponding boundary conditions on B (Eq. (??) and Eq. (??)):

Babove⊥ − B below⊥ = 0,andB above∥ − B below∥ = μ 0(K × n^). (4.30)
